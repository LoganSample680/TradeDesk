# Home improvement contract PRICE rules, 50 states plus DC

Gating data for the time-and-materials rebuild. The question this answers: for a
contractor working directly for a homeowner, what must the contract say about
PRICE, and is a time-and-materials or cost-plus contract permitted at all.

## Why this document exists

A widely repeated secondary source says six states require a home improvement
contract to show a total "in dollars and cents": California, Illinois,
Massachusetts, Nevada (pools), Pennsylvania and Tennessee. That list was quoted
into this project on 2026-09-17 and it is wrong on four of the six.

Checked against the statutes themselves:

  * Only CALIFORNIA uses the dollars-and-cents formula for the total.
  * ILLINOIS says "total cost". MASSACHUSETTS says "total amount agreed to be
    paid". TENNESSEE says "the agreed upon consideration", which is the loosest
    wording in the survey, not the strictest.
  * NEVADA's dollars-and-cents phrase governs the PAYMENT SCHEDULE on pool and
    spa contracts, not the total.
  * PENNSYLVANIA is the reverse of the claim. 73 P.S. 517.7 expressly
    contemplates a time-and-materials provision and prescribes its contents:
    the dollar value of the initial cost estimate, a statement that cost may
    not exceed 10% above it, and the total potential cost in actual dollars.
    That is a not-to-exceed cap written into statute. VERIFIED SEPARATELY
    against the statute text, not taken from the survey.

The list also misses states that genuinely do require a total dollar figure,
Arizona, Hawaii and Nevada among them.

## The three findings that drive the build

1. THREE TIERS, NOT ONE FLAG. Ten states require a total dollar figure; nine
   require a price OR a stated basis for calculating it; the rest require
   nothing about price. Kansas is in the last group and has no home improvement
   contract statute at all.
2. CALIFORNIA IS THE ONLY HARD NO. B&P 7159(d)(5) and 7159.5(a)(1) require a
   dollars-and-cents amount, CSLB guidance says contractors "are not allowed to
   use 'time and materials' or 'cost plus' contracts", and violating 7159.5 is a
   misdemeanor. Six more states (AZ, HI, NV, IL, MA, LA) cannot be satisfied by
   an open-ended T&M on the arithmetic, but no regulator is on record there.
   Warn, do not block.
3. PENNSYLVANIA AND VIRGINIA ALREADY SPECIFY THE NTE DOCUMENT. Build one cap
   template to PA 517.7(a)(8)(ii) and it satisfies Virginia's cost-plus cap
   requirement and Vermont's optional-maximum route as a side effect.

## How to read this

Every block states its own confidence. NOT CONFIRMED means the researcher could
not put eyes on a primary source, and those are listed together at the end.
Two commonly cited deposit rules, New Jersey's one-third and Delaware's
10%/33%, could NOT be substantiated in the statute or the regulation and are
probably repeated blog error. Do not enforce either without counsel.

This is research, not legal advice. Anything that becomes a hard block in the
product gets a lawyer's eyes first.

# 50-State + DC Survey: Price Terms Required in Residential Home Improvement Contracts

**Scope:** contractor working directly for a homeowner on an existing residence. Researched against primary sources (state legislature sites, licensing boards, state AG offices) where reachable; anything I could not put eyes on in a primary or regulator source is marked **NOT CONFIRMED**.

**Headline correction up front: the "six states require dollars and cents" list you were given is wrong on four of six.** Only California actually uses the phrase "dollars and cents" for the total. Illinois says "total cost," Massachusetts says "total amount agreed to be paid," Tennessee says "agreed upon consideration," and Pennsylvania expressly allows a time-and-materials contract instead of a total. Nevada's pool rule puts "dollars and cents" on the payment schedule, not the total. Meanwhile the list misses at least three states that genuinely do require a total dollar figure: **Arizona, Hawaii and Nevada (all residential work, not just pools)**, plus **Virginia** and **Indiana** on a slightly softer standard.

**Second headline: T&M and cost-plus are legal almost everywhere, and five states write them into the statute.** Pennsylvania, Vermont, Virginia, Maine, Wisconsin, Delaware, Minnesota and New Jersey all contemplate non-fixed pricing in the operative text. California is the only clear hard NO I could substantiate.

### CALIFORNIA
1. **Yes.** B&P Code §§ 7159, 7159.5 (home improvement); § 7151 defines home improvement.
2. **Yes, and this is the strictest in the country.** § 7159(d)(5): "The heading: 'Contract Price,' followed by the amount of the contract in dollars and cents." § 7159.5(a)(1): "The contract shall be in writing and shall include the agreed contract amount in dollars and cents." § 7159(d)(9) requires the progress payment schedule in dollars and cents too.
3. **NO. This is the hard no.** No statute says "cost-plus is prohibited" in those words, but a T&M or cost-plus contract cannot state a contract amount in dollars and cents, and CSLB treats that as dispositive. CSLB's disaster guidance is explicit: contractors "must use a fixed price contract" and "are not allowed to use 'time and materials' or 'cost plus' contracts," and CSLB notes post-disaster rebuilding is legally a home improvement project, so the same rules apply. Violation of § 7159.5 is a misdemeanor.
4. **Yes in practice, if the GMP is written as THE contract price in dollars and cents** and the job is completed for that amount. A "not to exceed $X, billed at cost" structure does not work, because the stated amount must be the contract amount, not a ceiling. **NOT CONFIRMED by CSLB in writing.**
5. Written contract required where the aggregate price "exceeds five hundred dollars ($500)."
6. **$1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less**, excluding finance charges. § 7159(d)(8) requires that exact sentence in caps in the contract. CSLB: "There are no exceptions for special order materials." Narrow exception for licensees with a blanket performance and payment bond.
7. Signed by the parties before work commences. **3 business days** right to cancel; **5 days** for senior citizens (§ 7159(e)(6)).

### DELAWARE

### PENNSYLVANIA

   - (ii)(A) the contract must state **the dollar value of that initial cost estimate**;
   - (ii)(B) it must state that **the cost "may not exceed 10% above the dollar value indicated in the initial cost estimate"**;

## SUMMARY (b): states where T&M or cost-plus appears impermissible

**Clear prohibition, regulator on record:**
- **California.** The only one. B&P 7159(d)(5) and 7159.5(a)(1) require a dollars-and-cents contract amount; CSLB's disaster guidance says contractors "must use a fixed price contract" and "are not allowed to use 'time and materials' or 'cost plus' contracts," and CSLB notes disaster rebuilding is legally home improvement so the rules are the same. Violation of 7159.5 is a misdemeanor.

**Probably impermissible by arithmetic, no regulator on record (all NOT CONFIRMED):**
- **Arizona** (total dollar amount including taxes), **Hawaii** (exact dollar amount due), **Nevada** (total amount to be paid), **Illinois** (total cost), **Massachusetts** (total amount agreed to be paid), **Louisiana** (total amount agreed to be paid). In each, a pure open-ended T&M cannot satisfy the text, but I found no board guidance, AG opinion or case law saying so. **Do not ship a hard block in these six without counsel.**

**Expressly permitted (build the feature for these):**
Pennsylvania, Vermont, Virginia, Maine, Wisconsin, Delaware, Minnesota, plus New Jersey / New York / Maryland / Tennessee by the "other consideration" wording, plus every no-statute state.

**Three states hand you the NTE design for free:**
- **Pennsylvania:** T&M must carry a written initial estimate, its dollar value in the contract, a statement that cost may not exceed 10% above it, the total potential cost "expressed in actual dollars," and a written-change-order requirement to go higher.
- **Virginia:** cost-plus must state the fee or percentage and "a cap that the total dollar amount cannot exceed."
- **Ohio:** the bill may not exceed the estimate by more than $5 or 10%, whichever is greater, without prior approval.
- **Vermont** is the mirror image: T&M **with or without** a maximum, both lawful.

## What this means for the feature, in four lines

1. **Do not build a single "total price required" flag.** Build three: *total dollars required* (10 states), *price-or-basis required* (9 states), *no price requirement* (the rest).
2. **T&M is legal nearly everywhere. Block it only in California**, and surface a warning (not a block) in AZ, HI, NV, IL, MA, LA.
3. **Pennsylvania and Virginia already specify the NTE document.** If you build one NTE/GMP template, build it to PA § 517.7(a)(8)(ii): initial estimate in dollars, the 10% ceiling language, the total potential cost in actual dollars, and the written change order clause. It satisfies Virginia's cost-plus cap and Vermont's option 2 as a side effect, and it is the §9.2 "NTE cap" backlog item nearly for free.
4. **Deposit caps are the highest-risk field.** Only 8 jurisdictions have a real cap and two of the commonly cited ones (NJ, DE) I could not substantiate. Get counsel on NJ before you enforce anything there.
